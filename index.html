Where It All Began

2016, I took on an off-campus apprenticeship and joined the National Laboratory of Pattern Recognition at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS). It was my very first exposure to a rigorous, fast-paced research environment. I was introduced to the foundational concepts of computational geometry and geometric modeling. I was immediately assigned to my first major project: Surface remeshing and segmentation.

Problem

Improving element quality in surface remeshing remains a major challenge. Traditional automatic remeshing algorithms often fail to preserve sharp and thin features. Existing segmentation methods also cannot efficiently eliminate poor-quality elements near boundaries.

Solution

We integrate local mesh operations, including vertex translation and edge flipping/splitting, into a segment-based remeshing pipeline. This approach generates high-quality, feature-preserving meshes with perfectly clean boundaries. It effectively eliminates poor-quality elements with negligible changes to the original mesh structure and complexity.

First .STEP into Industry Problem Solving

In 2017, I continued my research at the CAS, where I worked on a fully automated framework for tessellating industrial CAD models into high-quality triangular meshes. By combining Constrained Delaunay Triangulation (CDT) with CAD boundary representations (B-reps), we bridged parametric surfaces and polygonal meshes for downstream geometry processing. This project was my first experience with industry-scale CAD data, teaching me how theoretical geometry algorithms must be adapted to handle the complexity and imperfections of real engineering models.

Problem

Industrial CAD .STEP models often contain complex B-rep topologies, tiny features, and geometric degeneracies that make automatic tessellation unreliable. Existing approaches struggle to produce valid, simulation-ready meshes while simultaneously preserving geometric fidelity. Issues like invalid or even missing patches in triangulations are very common.

Solution

We designed a two-stage remeshing framework that reconstructs parametric domains, applies Constrained Delaunay Triangulation, and performs global mesh optimization with feature-aware remeshing. The pipeline automatically resolves common CAD degeneracies, preserves critical geometric features, and generates high-quality meshes suitable for engineering simulation.

Placing Humans in the Loop: VR & Perception

Entering my Ph.D. at Michigan Tech, my first project focuses on Virtual Reality (VR) and Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), aiming to understanding how people perceive and interact with virtual worlds. I engineered real-time interactive 3D environments and designed human-subject experiments to study egocentric distance perception in Head-Mounted Displays (HMDs). For the first time, my research centered around the human behavioral experiments.

Research Question

People often describe brightly lit spaces feeling larger, while dark spaces feel smaller. Would this intuition carry over into virtual reality? If a VR developer intentionally darkens an entire scene to create a particular atmosphere, would users become less accurate at judging distances?

Designing the Experiment

To answer this question, I designed a controlled human-subject study using direct blind walking, a well-established action-based measure of perceived distance. Participants first observed a virtual target, after which the display was removed and they attempted to walk to the remembered location while blindfolded. This method measures perceived distance through natural locomotion rather than verbal estimation.

Building the Experimental Platform

To conduct the study, I built a complete VR experimental platform. An 8 × 7 meter laboratory was instrumented with a 12-camera Vicon motion capture system, allowing sub-millimeter tracking of participants through reflective markers attached to the headset. The tracking data were synchronized with the VR application for accurate measurement of walking trajectories.

I also developed the virtual environment in OpenGL for the Oculus Rift CV1, recreating a real laboratory using photographic textures. Two experimental conditions were created: a normal scene and a dark scene whose luminance was reduced to only 1% of the original brightness, while keeping all geometric information unchanged.

What We Found

Across all tested distances, participants demonstrated nearly identical walking accuracy in both lighting conditions. Contrary to our intuition, dramatically reducing the overall brightness of a virtual scene DID NOT significantly affect perceived egocentric distance.

Why It Matters

Although the result appears simple, it carries practical implications for VR system design. Developers are safe to create dark virtual environments without compromising users' distance perception, enabling immersive nighttime scenes, underground facilities, or low-light training simulations.

For hardware manufacturers, the finding also suggests that reducing display brightness to save battery power is unlikely to impair spatial perception. More broadly, the work provides evidence that scene brightness can be treated as an independent design parameter rather than a factor that fundamentally alters distance judgments.

Turning Surfaces into Interfaces: Acoustic Keyboard for AR

After VR, my research led me to Augmented Reality (AR). The demand for a more natural input system has become increasingly evident as AR evolve. Traditional devices like keyboards and mice are often not suitable for immersive environments due to their reliance on bulky physical hardware and constrained interaction spaces. To address this, I developed a Python-based acoustic input system that turns ordinary flat surfaces into interactive interfaces by analyzing the propagation of sound generated by finger taps. By adapting Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) algorithms, this system provides a cost-effective and flexible alternative to conventional input methods.

The Core Challenge

Midair hand tracking and gesture recognition are common in AR/VR, but they often suffer from fatigue during prolonged use and a lack of tactile feedback. Surface-based interaction solves this by leveraging everyday flat surfaces (like desks and walls) to give users something physical to press against. The challenge was determining if we could use acoustic sensing, rely on listening for the vibrations caused by finger taps with strategically placed microphones to accurately localize interactions without needing visible cameras or body-worn equipment.

Algorithm Foundation

To detect and localize tap events, the system relies on the Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) method. When a tap occurs, the acoustic wave propagates outward and is captured by multiple microphones. By calculating the tiny time differences between when each pair of microphones hears the sound, the system uses hyperbolic equations to pinpoint the exact spatial offset of the user's interaction.

Building the Hardware Platform

To test this, I made a grid layout design and built a prototype system on a particleboard table covered with contact paper and a printed full-size ANSI keyboard in the center. I deployed eight piezoelectric contact microphones (four on the top surface and four directly underneath) to capture structure-borne acoustic signals. To ensure high temporal resolution for TDoA calculations, I focused on developing a robust 8*96kHz signal processing pipeline in Python, connecting the sensors to an external audio interface. For highly precise ground-truth validation, a Neonode infrared touch sensor was mounted directly over the keyboard area to capture millimeter-level 2D touch coordinates.

Signal Processing & Localization

Extracting the precise timestamp of a tap from background noise was crucial. The system applies a first-order Butterworth high-pass filter (130 Hz cutoff) to eliminate low-frequency environmental hum, followed by a Hilbert transform to extract the signal envelope for robust tap detection. For estimating the tap coordinates, I evaluated several localization solvers and ultimately implemented a customized Newton-Raphson iterative method. This method provided the lowest localization error while maintaining execution times under 10 ms, making it ideal for real-time responsiveness.

Results and Implications

Across structured user studies, the system proved reliable, more intuitive and widely preferred over a mid-air keyboard. We have a 90% dectection rate of all inputs. And in structured tasks, the median localization error remained close to 2.3 cm, which is accurate enough for autocorrect to work on.

These findings demonstrate that acoustic sensing is a highly viable path forward for VR/AR text entry. By effectively addressing noise robustness and precision timing, this work shows that we can successfully transform ordinary surfaces into responsive, tactile interfaces, drastically improving user comfort and input speed in immersive environments.
